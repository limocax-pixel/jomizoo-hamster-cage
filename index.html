The minimums the planner checks against. Each value links to its source; where guidelines differ, the sources list explains which value we used.

Minimums are a floor, not a goal: hamsters use every extra centimeter of space and depth. Only unbroken floor space counts — platforms, shelves and extra levels don't add to it.

How deep should hamster bedding be?

Hamsters are burrowing animals. In the wild they live underground in burrows with separate chambers for sleeping and for storing food, and pet hamsters keep the same instinct.

A controlled study kept 45 male Syrian hamsters on 10, 40 or 80 cm of bedding. Every hamster on 40 or 80 cm dug burrows. Those on 10 cm gnawed the cage wire significantly more and ran more in their wheels, and at 80 cm wire-gnawing was never seen. The authors concluded that at least 40 cm of bedding seemed to enhance welfare (Hauzenberger, Gebhardt-Henrich & Steiger, 2006). German veterinary guidance (TVT) asks for a diggable layer of at least 25 cm for Syrian hamsters and prefers 40 cm.

That's why the planner checks a minimum depth across the whole floor and highlights a deeper burrowing area. You don't have to fill the whole enclosure that deep: piling one section higher gives your hamster room to build a real burrow and keeps the amount of bedding manageable. Switch the 3D view to Front to see the tunnels, nest and food store appear as the bedding gets deeper.

How much bedding do I need?

liters = length × width × average depth (cm) ÷ 1,000

Working in inches? Multiply length × width × depth and divide the cubic inches by 61.02 to get liters.

With a deeper burrowing area, use the average depth: standard depth + (burrowing depth − standard depth) × share of the floor. For a 100 × 50 cm enclosure with 25 cm everywhere and 40 cm on 30% of the floor, the average is 29.5 cm, so you need 147.5 L of settled bedding.

Bedding compacts when it is pressed down to hold tunnels. Keepers report needing 1.5–2× the calculated volume in pack liters, so the planner adds 50% by default — adjust it to your bedding. Then divide by the expanded volume printed on the pack.

What size wheel does my hamster need?

A wheel is big enough when your hamster's back stays straight while running (TVT). If the back curves upward, the wheel is too small. Bigger is better: given the choice, Syrian hamsters preferred a 35 cm wheel to a 23 cm one (Reebs & St-Onge, 2005). Choose a solid running surface — wheels with rods caused paw wounds in a study (Beaulieu & Reebs, 2009).

Check that the wheel still fits once the bedding is in: the planner adds the bedding depth, a 2 cm stand and the wheel diameter and compares that with the enclosure height.

Tank, bin cage, wooden enclosure or wire cage?

  • Glass tanks hold deep bedding and let you watch the burrows through the glass. Use a mesh lid. Tank sizes are outside measurements: a 40-gallon breeder (about 91 × 46 cm) is below the 100 × 50 cm minimum, and a 75-gallon (about 122 × 46 cm) has enough area but is narrower than 50 cm.
  • Bin cages (large storage bins with mesh windows) are affordable, light and hold deep bedding well.
  • Wooden enclosures with an acrylic front are easy to build to any size and hold deep bedding.
  • Wire cages divide opinion: German veterinary guidance (TVT) considers them unsuitable, while the RSPCA prefers barred sides for ventilation. Bedding can only be as deep as the plastic base, and most pet-store models are below the minimum floor space.

How this planner works

  1. Floor space is length × width, compared with the species minimum. Platforms don't count. If the area is enough but the shortest side is under the minimum width, you get a warning.
  2. Height must meet the species minimum.
  3. Bedding depth across the floor must meet the species minimum. A deeper area counts as a full burrowing area at the depth listed in the table: 40 cm for Syrian hamsters, the depth at which every hamster in the 2006 study dug burrows.
  4. Wheel: the diameter must meet the species minimum, and bedding depth + 2 cm stand + wheel diameter must fit inside the enclosure height.
  5. Bedding volume uses the formula above, plus the compaction allowance you choose (50% by default). Packs are rounded up.
  6. Wire cages: bedding can't be deeper than the plastic base.
